Drywall Installation Service: What to Know Before You Hire

By Lloyd · Updated October 10, 2026 · 10 min read

Drywall installed on interior walls and ceilings

Hanging new drywall looks simple from the outside: boards go up, seams get covered, and the wall gets painted. In practice, a professional drywall installation service makes dozens of small decisions that determine whether your walls stay smooth and flat for years or show ridges, cracks, and nail pops within months. This guide explains how the work is done, what to expect, and how to hire well, so you can plan a remodel, an addition, or a basement finish with confidence.

If you are comparing contractors in Ypsilanti, Ann Arbor, Westland, Novi, or nearby Michigan communities, the same principles apply. The age of your home and the condition of the framing shape the right approach.

Key takeaways

  • Good drywall starts with straight, solid framing. Boards cannot fix a crooked wall.
  • Hanging, fastening, taping, and finishing are separate stages, and each one affects the final look.
  • Water damage, a sagging ceiling, or older materials that may contain asbestos call for caution before any work begins.
  • Compare scope, materials, and communication, not just the bottom line of a quote.

1. What a drywall installation service includes

A drywall installation service covers everything between bare framing and a wall that is ready for primer. Most projects include measuring and ordering, delivering and staging panels, cutting openings for outlets and windows, hanging boards, taping seams, applying joint compound, sanding, and cleanup. For a local example of a full-service scope, see drywall installation services in Ann Arbor, MI.

Residential drywall installation usually falls into a few project types:

  • New construction and additions, where every wall and ceiling is new.
  • Remodels, where walls are opened, moved, or replaced.
  • Basement and garage finishing, which often add moisture and code considerations.
  • Replacement after damage, such as water, demolition, or a failed older surface.

Hanging is only part of the job

The finishing stage, which means taping and coating seams in several thin layers, takes longer and has more effect on how the wall looks under light. Ask any provider how they approach finishing before you ask about hanging.

2. Drywall panel types and where they are used

Drywall panel installation begins with choosing the right panel. You may hear the phrase Sheetrock installation services, but Sheetrock is a brand name that many people use for all gypsum board. The type of panel matters more than the label on it.

  • Standard panels suit most dry interior walls and ceilings.
  • Moisture-resistant panels are often chosen for humid areas such as bathrooms. They are not a substitute for waterproofing, so follow the manufacturer's guidance and local code.
  • Fire-rated (Type X) panels are commonly used where building codes call for added fire resistance, such as some garage-to-house walls. Your plans and local code decide where they are required.

Thickness matters

Half-inch panels are common on residential walls, while five-eighths-inch panels are often used where extra strength, sag resistance, or fire resistance is needed.

3. Interior wall framing: the foundation of a flat wall

Interior wall framing sets the line your drywall will follow. Studs that are bowed, uneven, or inconsistently spaced create waves in the finished wall that no amount of compound can fully hide. Before panels go up, a careful installer checks that framing is plumb, straight, and properly spaced, and that blocking exists where edges and fixtures need support.

What to check before boards go up

  • Studs and joists are straight and flush along their faces.
  • Electrical, plumbing, and ductwork are finished, and any required inspections are passed before walls are closed.
  • Insulation is in place and dry.
  • Blocking is installed for cabinets, grab bars, shelves, and TV mounts.

When walls are being moved

If your project includes new partition walls, confirm who handles framing and any required permits. Structural or load-bearing changes should be reviewed by a qualified professional first.

4. Ceiling drywall installation: why it takes extra care

Ceiling drywall installation is harder than wall work for a simple reason: gravity. Panels are heavy, they must be lifted and held in place while fasteners are driven, and any weakness in the support shows up as sagging over time. Ceilings are typically hung before walls so the wall panels can tuck up against the ceiling edges.

  • Joist spacing and board thickness need to work together.
  • Light fixtures, fans, and vents need properly sized openings and support.
  • An existing ceiling with stains, sagging, or damage should be inspected before anything is installed over it.
Warning: sagging or stained ceilings. A bulging or stained ceiling can mean trapped water. Keep people and pets out from under it, and do not poke it. If water is near light fixtures or outlets, stay clear and call an electrician or your utility. Find and stop the source of moisture before any new drywall is installed.

5. Drywall hanging and fastening: what good work looks like

Drywall hanging and fastening follows a few basic rules that experienced crews apply on every job:

  • Panel edges land on framing members, and joints are staggered instead of lined up in a grid.
  • Screws are driven just below the paper surface without tearing it.
  • Panels fit snugly without being forced, and gaps are kept under control.
  • Openings for boxes and fixtures are measured, not guessed.
  • Fastener spacing follows the manufacturer's instructions and local code.
Tip: photograph the framing first. Before the drywall goes up, take photos of every wall with a tape measure visible. Later, they show where studs, wiring, and pipes sit when you want to hang shelves or a TV mount.

Why fastening affects appearance

Loose or over-driven fasteners can lead to nail pops and cracked seams as the house moves with the seasons. Consistent fastening keeps panels tight against the framing so finishing coats stay flat.

7. How a drywall installation project typically goes, step by step

  1. Walkthrough and measurements. The contractor visits, measures, and discusses the scope, panel types, and finish level.
  2. Written scope and schedule. You receive the plan in writing, including who handles removal, disposal, and cleanup.
  3. Site preparation. Floors and belongings are protected, and framing, wiring, plumbing, and insulation are confirmed ready.
  4. Delivery and staging. Panels are brought in and stored flat and dry near the work areas.
  5. Hanging. Ceilings go up first, then walls, with panels cut around openings and fastened to framing.
  6. Taping and first coat. Seams, corners, and fastener heads are covered with tape and compound.
  7. Additional coats and sanding. Thin coats are applied with drying time between them, then sanded smooth.
  8. Final inspection and cleanup. The surface is checked under good light, touched up, and cleaned for priming and paint.

Older homes: if your house was built before the 1980s, have older joint compound, textured ceilings, and plaster tested before they are disturbed. Homes built before 1978 may also have lead-based paint on surfaces that will be sanded or removed. Ask your contractor how they handle both.

8. What affects the cost of drywall installation

Every project is different, so a reliable quote comes from a site visit, not a generic estimate. These are the main factors that shape the cost:

  • Total area and the number of rooms.
  • Ceilings versus walls, and ceiling height.
  • Panel type and thickness.
  • Layout complexity, such as angles, arches, and many openings.
  • Condition of the framing and any corrections needed.
  • Removal and disposal of existing drywall.
  • Finish level and texture.
  • Site access and whether the home is occupied.

How to choose a drywall installation service provider

  • Ask for a written scope. It should name panel types, number of coats, finish level, and cleanup.
  • Confirm requirements. Check what licensing and insurance your city or state requires, and ask for proof instead of relying on a claim.
  • Look at past work. Photos of finished rooms show more than a sales pitch. You can also review a business's Google Business Profile for customer feedback.
  • Ask about dust control. Good crews protect floors, seal off work areas, and clean up daily.
  • Compare scope, not just totals. Two quotes only compare fairly when they include the same work.

Prevention tips to protect new drywall

  • Fix roof, plumbing, and window leaks promptly, and let wet areas dry fully.
  • Ventilate bathrooms and manage indoor humidity.
  • Keep drywall dry during delivery, storage, and installation.
  • Locate studs and use proper anchors for heavy items.
  • Watch small cracks and pops for growth, and address them early.

Drywall installation FAQs

How long does a drywall installation take?

Hanging the panels can move quickly, but finishing needs drying time between coats of joint compound. Even a small room usually takes several days from start to primer-ready. A contractor can give a realistic schedule after seeing the space.

Can new drywall be installed over old drywall?

In some cases, yes, but only when the existing board is dry, sound, and firmly fastened. Damaged, wet, or crumbling drywall should be removed first. Adding a layer can also change door and window trim details and electrical box depth, so ask your installer how they would handle it.

What is the difference between Sheetrock and drywall?

Drywall, also called gypsum board, is the product. Sheetrock is a brand name of drywall that many people use as a general term. When you ask for Sheetrock installation services, you are usually asking for standard drywall installation.

Do I need to leave my home during drywall work?

Not always, but expect noise and fine dust, especially during sanding. Ask how the crew will protect floors, seal off work areas, and clean up. People with asthma or other breathing concerns may want to stay elsewhere while sanding is under way.

Should I test for asbestos before drywall work in an older home?

If your home was built before the 1980s, older joint compound, textured ceilings, or plaster may contain asbestos. Do not sand, scrape, or cut these materials until they have been tested by a qualified professional or accredited lab, and follow your local rules for handling and disposal.
